[00:00] What if your website passes every accessibility check and still drives people away? Isn't this already covered by accessibility? That's the question I hear most often when I bring up neurodivergent experience design. On paper, it isn't a bad question. It's meant to be practical. It implies that someone has already done the work. But the truth is, no, it isn't fully covered. Accessibility guidelines focus heavily on visible or physical impairments. 

[00:32] Screen reader compatibility, color contrast, keyboard navigation, closed captions, those things are essential, but they aren't enough. Cognitive accessibility is a different beast. Most digital systems, audits, and standards still don't know how to measure it, let alone how to design for it. Consider my own experience with the BBC. A while ago, the BBC redesigned its website to improve accessibility. Bigger 

[01:03] fonts, higher contrast, simpler layouts. On paper, it was perfect. But for me, it broke the experience. Something about the spacing, rhythm, and flow made it unreadable, not visually, but cognitively. My brain couldn't track it. I lost my place. I couldn't skim. I had to read one line at a time with frequent breaks. Eventually, I stopped using the website, not out of protest, out of exhaustion. I didn't complain. I didn't post about it. 

[01:36] I didn't write an open letter. I simply left. That's what many neurodivergent users do. We disappear quietly because we don't have the energy to explain why. Real accessibility, especially for neurodivergent people, isn't just about whether we can do something. It's about how hard it is to do it. Cognitive load is the hidden tax. It's the extra seconds we spend trying to find a button, the re-reading, the backtracking, the sensory overwhelm, the 

[02:07] constant self-correction. Real accessibility means not having to read every line twice. It means knowing where your eyes should go next. It means having clear contrast that doesn't cause strain. It means not being startled by movement, sound, or modal interruptions. It means having the option to move slowly or quickly without pressure. These things aren't nice to have. They're survival tools for staying 

[02:38] present. Imagine you design a checkout process that passes every web content accessibility guideline standard. It's keyboard navigable. It has labels. It scales responsively. But you've split the process into five tiny steps, each with a surprise validation field. A summary box updates in real time. A sticky footer containing a live chat slides onto the screen. A complete your purchase timer ticks away in the corner. 

[03:08] From a compliance standpoint, it's accessible. From a cognitive standpoint, it's chaos. For someone with ADHD, autism, or trauma sensitivity, it may be the last thing they try on your website. Let's be blunt. Neurodivergent users don't just want comfort. We represent real conversion risk and opportunity. About one in five people is neurodivergent. Some studies suggest the number may be higher when undiagnosed adults are 

[03:38] included. Neurodivergent people are also more likely to prefer online experiences over in-person ones. We may avoid overstimulating environments. We value control. We often shop at night. We want to do things in our own way. What happens when your website interrupts us? What happens when your product makes too many assumptions? What happens when your content creates overwhelm? We leave. No warning, no feedback. Just a bounce. 

[04:09] That bounce rate you can't explain. That conversion leak. That mysterious abandonment. It might be us. It's no coincidence that many neurodivergent users form deep bonds with AI tools. Large language models such as chat GPT offer cognitive grace. They tolerate ambiguity. They accept partial questions. They let you repeat yourself. They don't time out in the middle of a thought. They summarize. They simplify. They don't scold. That isn't just user 

[04:41] experience. That's emotional safety. When I use Mac, my AI, I don't worry about phrasing everything perfectly. I don't become flustered if I can't remember a term. I can say, "That thing involving dopamine and motivation." And Mac understands what I mean. That's what accessibility feels like. Accessibility checklists are helpful, but they become dangerous when they're treated as the finish line. A compliance system can still be unusable. A tested interface 

[05:12] can still trigger anxiety. A universal design can still exclude people. Inclusion isn't a checkbox. It's a posture. If your process doesn't include neurodivergent testing, feedback, and iteration, it isn't inclusive. It's incomplete. Here's the good news. Designing for cognitive access makes everything better. Clear structure. Reduced noise. Predictable flows. Graceful error handling. All of these things help every user, not 

[05:44] only those who identify as neurodivergent. If you want a competitive advantage in retention, conversion, and loyalty, start here. Because when neurodivergent users feel seen, we stay, we evangelize, we trust, we return, not out of convenience, out of relief.

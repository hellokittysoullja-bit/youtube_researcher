It's called body doubling. So, what exactly is a body double? At its core, body doubling is a gentle, passive form of accountability. It simply means doing a task while someone else is present, either in person or virtually, so you're less likely to get distracted. That person 

[00:32] isn't supervising you. They're not checking your work. They're not holding you accountable in an active way. Their presence alone acts as an anchor for your attention. And for the ADHD brain, that matters a lot more than most people realize. From a neurological standpoint, ADHD is not just about attention. It's about self-regulation. The ADHD brain has a harder time internally generating structure, urgency, and task salience, especially for tasks that are boring, repetitive, long, or mentally demanding. 

[01:03] This has a lot to do with dopamine. Dopamine isn't just a motivation chemical. It plays a major role in anticipation, task initiation, and sustained effort. When dopamine signaling is low or inconsistent, which is common in ADHD, tasks that don't offer immediate reward feel disproportionately hard to start and even harder to stick with. This is where body doubling comes in. When another person is present, the brain receives external cues that increase alertness and engagement. There's subtle social 

[01:33] stimulation, a mild sense of structure, and a clearer signal of this is work time. All of that helps stabilize dopamine signaling and reduces the amount of internal effort required to stay focused. In other words, you're no longer relying solely on your own executive function to hold everything together. You're borrowing structure from your environment. This is also why many people with ADHD notice they work better in coffee shops, libraries, or shared workspaces, even if they're not interacting with anyone. The nervous 

[02:04] system is picking up on the presence of others who are engaged and focused and it naturally mirrors that state. Body doubling is especially helpful for tasks like homework or studying, writing papers or emails, administrative work, long projects, or anything you've been procrastinating on because it feels overwhelming or tedious. Another important benefit of body doubling is that it reduces cognitive load. When you're working alone, your brain is doing two jobs at once. Number one is doing the task and number two is

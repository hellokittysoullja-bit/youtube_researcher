Here are 10 things to avoid or remove from your UX resume. Number 10, photography and headshots. Many studies have shown that resumes have lots of problems with unconscious bias. That means some people receive fewer callbacks from their job applications due to who they are 

[00:31] and what they look like and not what they can do. Never use a headshot in a resume unless applying to jobs in a country with cultural expectations of including one. Number nine, professional references. While it's wonderful to have people who will vouch for you and your work, it's unnecessary to list their names or mention that they're available upon requests on your resume. Number eight, hobbies and volunteering. Hey, rock climbing. Helping a local animal shelter 

[01:02] or spending time with the family are great pastimes, but how we spend our free time is irrelevant to future employers who are trying to determine if you can successfully do a job. Also, be aware that some interests may increase unconscious bias by indirectly signaling your identity to the hiring manager. Number seven, mission or objective statements. These statements are sometimes put at the top of the resume to describe desired roles, projects 

[01:32] or focus areas. But competent hiring managers want factual content grounded in context and objective results. These statements frequently focus on what the candidate wants from their next job and not what they can contribute. It's uninformative and not worth the space. Number six, school or personal projects. Your design portfolio is the best medium to document projects. Listing them separately on your resume is a filler tactic. 

[02:05] Instead of a project section, describe the project's impact and organize them under the work or educational experience where they happened. Number five, inaccessible portfolio links. The hiring manager will be looking for a portfolio. For website hosted portfolios, double check that the link in the resume works and that a password is included in a cover letter or elsewhere on the application form if it's password protected. Number four, errors. I've seen several resumes describing 

[02:36] pixel perfect attention to detail, and yet had a spelling or grammar mistake in that very same sentence. Errors in resumes or other professional documents imply carelessness with our deliverables, especially when there's ample time to proofread them and correct mistakes. Number three, excessive visual design. Even for designers skilled in visual design, don't let background fills, iconography, low contrast typography, employer logos or other graphics, complicate your resume. 

[03:09] Prioritize substance over style by using your resume to describe career achievements. Number two, subheadings. Subheadings are great for structuring webpage content and help with scanning but resumes should only contain three things. Work experience, education, and a few contact details. Ditch the subheadings and reclaim the space. And the number one thing to avoid in UX resumes, listing out skills, apps or technologies. 

[03:42] These lists are a popular way to cram many keywords into a resume. Sometimes they're presented as bar charts or unit charts that convey mastery of a particular tool or skill. And while they may look striking, the numbers are subjective. These lists are easy to make and they fill up space if the resume lacks content. They also try to manipulate the resume scanning software many employers use by containing a high number of keywords. But these lists are ultimately filler 

[04:13] to a human hiring manager. They don't convey when or where you use that skill or technology, and most importantly, they don't describe your impact or results. Don't let these fluffy and unhelpful types of content hurt your resume's performance. Fill all that new space with how you made a difference where you worked.

[03:06] keep the flow of content unbroken. At the heart of infinite scrolling lies a simple but powerful mechanism. As the user approaches the end of what's currently visible, the system quietly prepares the next batch of data. Modern browsers use something called Intersection Observer to watch for a sentinel element, a marker placed near the bottom of the feed. When this marker enters the viewport, the browser triggers a fetch for more content, often without the user noticing any delay. This approach, known as lazy 

[03:38] loading, means the page never truly ends. It just keeps extending one invisible threshold at a time. To maintain a seamless experience, developers rely on virtualization strategies. Instead of loading hundreds or thousands of items into the browser's memory, only the posts or videos currently visible and a small buffer above and below are actually rendered. As the user scrolls, old items are recycled or discarded, and new ones are created on the fly. 

[04:10] This keeps performance smooth, even on older devices or slow connections. Frameworks like React Window and libraries built for mobile apps manage this recycling behind the scenes, allowing the feed to appear endless without overwhelming the device. Every detail is tuned for continuity. The scroll bar adjusts dynamically, giving the illusion of a single uninterrupted stream. There are no hard stops, no next page buttons, no loading screens to break the spell. Even the 

[04:41] network requests are staggered, so the next set of posts is ready before the user ever reaches the bottom. On platforms like Facebook or Instagram, this means the feed can stretch for hours, always staying just ahead of the user's finger. The removal of stopping cues is not just a side effect, it is a deliberate technical choice. By erasing the natural breaks that once signaled an end, the system encourages users to keep moving forward. The underlying code is invisible, but its impact is felt every time a new 

[05:11] story appears without interruption. What looks like a simple scroll is, in reality, a carefully engineered environment where the only limit is the user's willingness to continue. [05:42] The brain craves novelty and surprise, and infinite scroll delivers both through unpredictable bursts of content. Sometimes the next post is a meme, sometimes it is breaking news. [06:12] This effect is explained by what researchers call a variable ratio schedule, the same principle that keeps gamblers pulling the lever, never knowing when the next reward will come. To measure the impact, scientists developed the binge scrolling scale, a validated tool that tracks compulsion, loss of control, and preoccupation with feeds. High scores on this scale match up with longer time spent scrolling, showing just how effective unpredictable rewards are at driving persistent use.

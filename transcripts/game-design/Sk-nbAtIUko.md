# How Gameplay Loops Keep You Playing

The entire catalogue of indie darling Supergiant falls into this category for me and their most recent title, Hades, which is still in early access, is an excellent example of a game I just couldn’t put down. Hades sees you playing as Zagreus, son of the eponymous lord of the underworld, who’s trying to escape hell. In Hades, as in a lot of roguelikes, gameplay is structured in a familiar pattern. First, you start in hell with only some basic

[01:38] In game actual design terms used by actual game developers, which is to say not me this is what’s called a gameplay loop, a series of repeated actions or emotional states that serves as the foundation for everything you do in the game. Gameplay loops can be found all over the place, from shooters, to RPGs, to narrative games and roguelikes, they’re a pretty foundational game design lesson and I think it’s really important to learn about what they do and why they work to understand why particular games can keep our attention and why some end up languishing in the backlog, as well as some other reasons that will… 

[02:11] become apparent later. Hades’ cycle of death and rebirth is far from its only gameplay loop,- in fact, most games operate using multiple loops of different lengths and scales with some being as short as seconds. For example,in the equally hellish DOOM, the main gameplay loop of entering a room, shooting bad guys until they’re all dead, grabbing whatever they dropped and zooming off to the next pack can resolve multiple times a minute and as anyone who’s played doom before will know, this particular sequence of events makes up most of your playtime. These quick, mechanically intensive loops that you spend a lot of time directly engaging 

[02:45] with are often called core loops, which is a phrase you may have heard before. It’s very important to make sure a games foundational mechanical interactions feel fun to use, because you’ll be going through the motions of their core loops maybe hundreds of times in any given play session. A single platforming challenge with an approach, execution and recovery in Mario, is a core loop older than a significant percentage of people watching this video, and nintendo have famously always gone to great lengths to make sure that Mario is fun to simply move around, because that’s what you’ll spend 99% of your time doing. The different challenges 

[03:17] themselves are just layers on top of the same loop, so if the underlying gameplay isn’t fun, you’ll get bored of the different mechanical gimmicks on display no matter how creative. This is why rubbish platformers back in the day that tried to copy Mario ended up failing, they copied the larger loops and overall structure, but didn’t spend enough time making the core loop of running and jumping any fun. Short and snappy core loops are what keep players engaged in the short term, and the inherent satisfaction of pulling each step off helps them to internalise the fundamental gameplay patterns that developers expect them to know off by heart. The ideal core loop, weirdly, is one that player rarely actually thinks about. In the best shmups, you blast formations of bad guys on instinct, and great action games train players to act quickly 

[03:50] and rely on their knowledge of attack patterns and combos- two core loops to get them through each fight. Core loops exists as a constant foundation that underpins everything else, that the game can always return to after finishing with bigger stuff. However, for much larger, longer gameplay loops, the opposite is true. Because a single cycle might take hours to resolve, it’s much harder to directly tie it to the mechanics. Take the cycle of conquest in a strategy game like total war. First, you pick where you want to invade, muster your armies, duke it out with the enemy, either win or lose the 

[04:22] war and then either cement your hold on your new territory or slink back home to regroup. You might only get through one or two of these loops in a given play session, and it’s completely disconnected from the moment-to-moment gameplay, which is mostly clicking through menus or telling your dudes which orcs to shoot. Longer gameplay loops are much more concerned with offering an abstract source of motivation and something to come back to, rather than immediate fun. Old-school JRPGs turn a lot of people off because their core loop, that being individual turns in a combat encounter are usually pretty boring. However, other people love RPGs because they place a massive 

[04:55] emphasis on high-level meta gameplay loops, where you get to see your characters and the story evolve over tens of hours, and the smaller gameplay loops are just steps towards that eventual goal. By offering up plot mysteries and the opportunity to see character growth, games can not only keep you entertained, but also give you a goal to shoot for, and the need to experience the satisfying resolution to this loop is something that will bring you back to the game after the more immediate thrill of the gameplay has faded. It’s by learning why gameplay loops work, and how they relate to player motivation, that we can understand that the problem of unengaging games is actually a two-pronged 

[05:28] issue. The more emphasis a game places on short gameplay loops, the more fun it is in the moment, but it’s going to be more difficult to give players a reason to come back. Games with very long gameplay loops that delay satisfaction, on the other hand need you to put a lot of effort in before you start having fun and so they end up in the I’ll get around to it one day pile forever. So, how do compelling games fix both of these problems at the same time, well, more often than not, that special breed of endlessly playable games organise their entire structure around one or occasionally two medium-length gameplay loops supported by longer and shorter 

[06:03] ones. This ensures that gameplay has a predictable rhythm and structure whilst still giving players a nice amount of short term goals to direct them to where the fun stuff is. For all the hate I and other people have given them over the years, stuff like towers and bases to capture, overused though they may be, serve as anchors for the medium length gameplay loop of exploring a new area, conquering it, then moving on. Just imagine if breath of the wild had no shrines, or if far cry had no cult outposts, you’d have vast swathes of the game where there’s nothing to do but travel to the next story segment, and 

[06:33] it’d be really hard to get into a nice consistent gameplay groove. The trope of splitting up games into themed areas where players engage with the same central gameplay loop eight times because it’s always eight for some reason, is also an example of this tactic. As much as people claim to want total freedom, the sheer directionlessness of a hypothetical open world pokemon game for example would more often than not lead to people getting lost, confused and critically bored before they’d completed the game. But, by splitting things up into 8 gym leaders and one or two crime syndicates, each containing 

[07:04] a number of goes around the core battling loop, devs can ensure that their players don’t feel overwhelmed as they slowly progress towards completing the next big story loop, which are usually capped off with rival battle bossfight. A lot of games, rather than having a single main loop, have two main loops instead that each feed into each other. Steamworld dig 2 is a great example of this particular style, with the entire structure of the game being built around the dual loops of mining in the caves, and then upgrading back in town. Here’s how it works. As you adventure deeper and deeper into the mines of El Machino, mining minerals and killing baddies, eventually, 

[07:38] your pack will fill up or your torch will run out, and you’ll be forced to return to the surface. This means you’re invariably going to have to say goodbye to some precious goodies or an unexplored part of the dungeon. Once you get to the surface, you can sell your haul to buy fancy new upgrades but things are balanced in such a way that you’ll never quiiite have enough to buy everything you want, giving you a new goal to shoot for as you drop back into the mines in search of that loot you abandoned before in a constant cycle of effort and rewards, and new opportunities. This loop in steamworld dig plays out once every 10 or so minutes and is broken up occasionally 

[08:09] by an optional puzzle dungeon, with entire zones taking about an hour long play session to fully traverse. Having an ideal session length in mind when developing a game can be very helpful for figuring out how long gameplay loops should be. Ideally, you want a play session to last for about as long as a single big, meta loop, and to get in a few rotations of medium length loops in along the way. By following this structure, each play session can feel like a satisfying experience, but there’s always something new to do for when you come back. Pikmin is fantastic at providing a constant sense of progress contained within a rhythmic 

[08:41] central gameplay loop. The strict schedule imposed by pikmin’s day-based structure is the key to it’s endlessly playable design: each morning you’ll start off by replenishing your forces from the previous day’s losses, the afternoon will be spent forging into new terrain, fighting bosses or finding loot, and the evening will be spent in a mad dash to bring all your ill gotten gains back to your ship before night falls and nocturnal predators devour any pikmin left outside. Your average play session of an hour or two is enough to get in maybe five or six days, which is about enough time to complete or at least fully explore a single area. 

[09:13] As a second example, Factorio has a much longer session length of a few hours, and from experience, they’re usually paced around each technological milestone. Building up to unlocking the next type of research takes around an entire play session, with smaller objectives like researching other techs, developing production line expansions and alien genocide missions to keep you engaged along the way. Even games with tiny play lengths like Devil Daggers still follow this structure, each one to two minute long playthrough is split into distinct phases, you’ve got the intro with the squid towers, then you’ve got your first upgrade and the first spider, then the 

[09:45] second upgrade and your first centipede, etcetera, this helps to ground what’s otherwise a very chaotic game with an intense core loop in some sort of structure, and makes marking your progress as well as creating goals for yourself much much easier. Gameplay loops are powerful things, and as we’ve already seen, by using them in the right way and leveraging the inherent structure of video games to our advantage, we can make great games even more fun and satisfying to play, and encourage people to stick with titles that might take a little while to really get going. However, there is a flipside to this, 

[10:17] and that’s the fact that just like how an understanding of gameplay loops can turn good games into great ones, gameplay loops can also turn a game that’s merely bad, into an exploitative, addictive one. Basically any crappy mobile game that tries to swindle you out of your cash uses gameplay loops to manipulate you into playing, and more importantly paying more. Candy crush’s core loop of matching things is flashy and satisfying, there’s lots of bars to fill up and levels to beat to give you a sense of progress, and the whole thing is divided into arbitrary milestones to get you to come back. These are the same tactics used in games 

[10:48] that are actually good, like, you know, bejeweled, but here the motivation is to trick you into buying microtransactions by deliberately cutting loops short and making you feel unsatisfied until you pay to keep playing. This is kind of my motivation for making this video, as well as a lot of others on the channel - gameplay loops are a fact of game design, and that’s a good thing! But equally, gameplay loops can fool us into wasting time on games that we don’t actually enjoy. No disrespect intended towards those who do like the game, but I’m not a fan of Destiny 2. By understanding how games and their mechanics work, not only can we better appreciate the titles we love, but we can also learn to make more informed decisions about which games we play and why. For every Hades, Steamworld dig or Devil daggers, there are a million crappy games wanting to steal your money and your time. The only way to distinguish the 

[11:53] two is to occasionally take a step back, ask yourself whether you’re genuinely having fun, or just going through the motions of a gameplay loop - if the answer is the latter, it’s probably time to stop and do something else.

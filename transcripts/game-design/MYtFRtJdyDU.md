[00:00] Hi everybody, my name is Daniel Zaidan. I'm a Game Designer here at TDZ Games and welcome to our second Indie Dev Log. Today we're gonna talk about one of the coolest processes in game development: Level Design! So in the beginning of the development process there was no TDZ Games, it was just me trying to learn how to develop games. So I did what most people do and start looking for tutorials. So I learned how to model and animate and program and do all that stuff. 

[00:32] And I put together a quick prototype. It looked pretty good and it had an enemy and platform challenges but it wasn't quite fun. So I started to do research and found out about the Game Design so I started to study that and learn that. This is the first book that I read, Level up: The Guide to Great Video Game Design by Scott Rogers. And it really put me in the right step to start exploring this area. So I was learning Game Design and Level Design while creating the game. That made it possible to create a lot of cool stuff. One of the things that I implemented right off the bat was the use of fingers. 

[01:03] In the book he explains that fingers are tracks off the main path that the player can explore and maybe find some rewards. In the swamp level I created this path off the main track that player could explore if they find it and find a reward at the end. In the city level I decided to create something more challenging, so the player has to execute a trick maneuver... ...and if they do get there they'll get the reward and they go back to the main track or maybe a different track. Scott Rogers really like to compare Level Design to what people do at Disneyland, the way they guide the visitor there and the guests. 

[01:34] So, he has a great talk at GDC that talks about it and the book too. So one of the things that he explains is the concept of weenies and by the way Mark Brown has a great video on it, I really suggests you watch it. The idea is that weenies are references that the players have in the level that can guide them without the use of a map for instance, that's pretty cool. Another great place that I learned a lot about game and level design was Extra Credit's Side Quest about Dark Souls. Dan and James played the game observing what aspects worked and didn't work in the game's design. One of the things that I learned and thought that was amazing is like how they used the items that you could collect to show you the possibilities of the world. 

[02:10] So maybe you were in this path but you could see far off an item and you were like: "Ok, so maybe I could get there at some point". So I found out that a lot of games use that and it's awesome. One of them is Crash, Crash Bandicoot; that is a great reference to Eliosi's Hunt. So in this specific area as you play through the platforming levels you can see back there, there's a crystal and you want to collect that. You don't know how you can do it quite yet but it's there. So I tried to implement that in a couple of ways into the game. In the desert level there's a flame thrower off a little bit of the side. So if you do notice it you'll go back and think:... 

[02:42] "Oh, maybe I can go through a different way and find that flame thrower or something else, maybe an enemy, a reward, another path..." That's also used in like the city level, where you see the map and you can't quite reach it, you have to find another way but you don't know how. That's interesting because the player feel that the world is bigger and it worked really well. So as I was developing the sketches for the level design I found out that there were a couple of ways to improve that. One of those is to write down everything you have in mind. That way it will be way clearer for when you have complex designs or monsters or anything like that, so I started to do that in later designs. 

[03:17] Another thing is to use reference scales, so maybe do that in a paper with what one meter looks like, so it can be more faithful to the final result. So after the basics sketches I implemented that in the level design and as The Art of Game Design says... ...“The more times you test and improve your design, the better your game will be”. This means that when you do implement the level design you are going to find a lot of things to change... ...or not, but mostly you will. That's what happened to the desert level. As we where testing the level we found out that it wasn't quite ready, that was something missing... We couldn't quite put our finger on it. Then, I learned about interest curves. 

[03:49] Extra Credits has a great video on it, I really suggest to watch it, but it's really just an introduction to the topic. The Art of Game design has a great, whole chapter about it, that's really cool. So analyzing it I found that the beginning was pretty cool, that had a great hook... ...that get players excited about the level and the climax was great, with a great boss battle. So in the middle, was falling flat. So I decided to add two encounters there and it made a perfect addition to the level. With every iteration I was learning what made the game better and better, so by the end of it I was creating designs that really pushed the experience to it's limits. 

[04:23] There's a great article in Gamasutra, where the author provides ways to study and evaluate level design and he also compare these metrics to music theory. That's great, because as we learn from other medium we can start to explore our own. That's the interesting thing about level and game design as a whole, is that there's still a lot of things to discover and learn. Jonathan Blow created a prototype a couple of years ago where he tried to explore how the changes in the mechanics made the player feel. That's similar to how a composer changes the rhythm and melody of a song, for instance, to make the listener feel a specific way.

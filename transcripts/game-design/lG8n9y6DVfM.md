# On Killing Vaas Montenegro in Far Cry 3 | Game Narrative Design
Tushar Deb. · 9 мин · 11338 просмотров · en · https://youtu.be/lG8n9y6DVfM



[00:00] Hi. I wanna talk about player and character motivations. The motivations of you, the player, and the main in-game controllable character in a game. And how when both the player and character motivations get too apart from each other the in-game experiences become rather so bland and shallow. And sometimes even so uninspiring that the motive to keep making progress in the game or the storyline is hindered. I wanna talk about one of the most disappointing or rather sad moments in video games when only to make progress in Far Cry 3, 

[00:31] you had to kill Vaas. Let’s cut to the chase, the biggest, if not the only, reason why most of us didn’t want to kill Vaas was because Vaas is the G.O.A.T. The very first few minutes of our first interaction with Vaas were just so enough to fall for the character. Vaas: "I like this phone, this is a nice fucking phone." Over the game’s buildup and with each interaction with Vaas, 

[01:03] I personally started to really develop a fondness for him, a fondness that got deeper than what I would develop for any other character in the game; or even other games. We, players, start to long for the next interaction with Vaas. To witness another mind-blowing and captivating performance. And with each interaction we discover a little more about what Vaas really is, how he thinks and what his overall personality is, which is very out of the ordinary. We start to like, if not obsess over, Vaas. And our this fondness for Vaas, or mine personally at least, 

[01:35] overpowered my empathy for Jason. The character I am actually controlling, experiencing the world through and the character I am maybe meant to relate with more. And this is exactly where the player’s and the character’s motivations split into two. Jason absolutely hates Vaas and wants to kill him. And why should he want otherwise? Vaas captured him and his friends, shot his brother in the neck, and tried to kill him multiple times. But most of us, the players, don’t even hate Vaas at all. In fact we admire him for what he really is. 

[02:06] We want more of him. We want to know more about him. But Jason wants him to die already. In his well-written and thought-provoking article Alexander Freed writes, “..the more a player’s motivations diverge from those of the player character, the more difficult it is for the player to empathize and immerse herself in the player character’s head... If I like an antagonist the player character loathes, my interest in overcoming a thousand obstacles to defeat the enemy dwindles.” Look at how furious Jason is when confronting Vaas. 

[02:37] Jason: "VAAS!" "VAAS! WHERE ARE YOU!" But are we, the players, just as furious and motivated to kill Vaas? Absolutely not. At least most of us aren’t. I remember I was so shocked and taken aback and had to spend a few minutes away from the game when I realised killing Vaas is the only way to make progress in the storyline. Though, maybe necessary for Jason to escape the island, killing Vaas was so unsatisfying because We, the players, didn’t want him to die in the first place. The game required us to do something the character wanted or needed to do, 

[03:09] which was completely opposite of what the player would have liked more. It is because the player’s and the character’s motivations got so far apart that the experience of killing Vaas and trying to complete the storyline after Vaas is gone, to many of us, feels very shallow and amiss. If I have reached a point where I am more upset about the loss of a character’s foe, more than I am happy or delighted about the character’s progress towards reaching its ultimate goal, it clearly conveys how I as the player have barely built up any empathy for the character in the first place. 

[03:42] If my, the player’s, and the in-game character’s motivations were rather so unified, I would have been more happy at the death of Vaas and would have been too excited for what’s to come next, both for Jason, the character and me, the player. Kirk Hamilton writes in his Kotaku article, “The best character in Far Cry 3 isn't Jason, nor any of his friends. The real star of Far Cry 3 is Vaas.” In another article Thomas Ella writes, “Once Vaas is gone, though, the game loses its magic… 

[04:12] Far Cry 3 lives and dies by Vaas.” What I am really trying to say is that the narrative of Far Cry 3 didn’t quite do well in nurturing the player’s empathy for the main character. We might have even progressed and completed the game but it wasn’t ‘cause we really cared about Jason succeeding or that we really empathized with or felt sorry for his situation. We really kept going cause we were meant to finish the game or maybe just ‘cause the gameplay was fun. At least I progressed for those reasons. 

[04:42] though always hoping that Vaas might return. Now, I’m not saying the game is poor just because the game didn’t manage to unify the player and character motivations but what I am definitely trying to say is that when the character and player motivations are unified they give birth to a more pleasing, satisfying and unforgettable experience. I remember, strangely on my second playthrough of Mafia II, I developed quite a fondness for Henry Tomasino. An NPC in the game who becomes quite a good friend of Vito, 

[05:13] the main controllable protagonist and their mutual friend, Joe. The man always dressed sharp and something in him was very likeable; but as the storyline unfolds, on our way to see Henry one morning at Lincoln Park, we discover him being brutally attacked with meat cleavers by a few members of a criminal organization. Maybe it was the brutal way to attack someone, or the brutal way Henry was butchered that really broke my heart and absolutely infuriated me. I got upset and I really genuinely felt what the main character and his friend were feeling. 

[05:45] I wanted to avenge Henry. And the next thing I know, Vito and Joe do the exact thing that I wanted to do in the game right then. Vito and Joe track the crime boss that ordered Henry’s death, kill all his men and put a bullet in his head I remember I was so satisfied when we took down the crime boss; but it was because for that very moment the in-game character’s and my, the player’s, motivations were absolutely unified. I was upset and furious at the butchering of a character I liked, and the in-game characters were absolutely upset and furious for the same reason. 

[06:17] I wanted to avenge Henry and that’s what the in-game characters wanted to do as well. I wanted to do it right away and that’s what the character’s did too. Without gathering backup, or even discussing it with their bosses, they went right in. Or in fact, we went right in. And such moments with unified motivations are very moving and intimate. You don’t wait for the next objective, or goal, to be assigned to you but it comes naturally to you. That makes you feel you are not progressing how the game wants you to 

[06:48] but the game is progressing based on how you want to. Now I understand such an experience could be rather subjective; You might not have, or may not, develop a fondness for a character like Henry. But you get a taste of how the experience is when the player and character motivations unite. Which is also how Jason’s next steps and thoughts would have come naturally to us, the players, had there been more about him to relate with. Killing Vaas would have been so satisfying if it were Jason who we really empathised with. We would have forgotten about Vaas if it were really Jason 

[07:21] who would have occupied our thoughts. Unlike movies, where motivations of the viewer and the protagonist could be detached but you may still watch the storyline from the space of a third person witnessing all that’s going about the character’s life and judging it’s choices and decisions, it is you, the player, making choices and decisions in the game for the character. And so the motivations are necessary for the player to keep making progress and make choices better for the character. And if the game manages to unite the motivations of the player and the character, 

[07:52] there is way more chance that the player will relate even more with the character, think like and even experience the story the way the character is written to be experiencing. Then not only the motivations unite, but the thinking, decisions and the choices come naturally to the player; close to matching what the character would choose itself. And then the game doesn’t necessarily have to visually notify it to the player; Which makes the in-game experience much more immersive. Alexander Freed also writes in his article, “bridging the gap between player motivation and player character motivation 

[08:26] is one of the most important factors in a game narrative’s success.” And player and character motivations don’t really begin unified with the start of a new game; That rarely happens. It is something that the game has to rather bring to life through gameplay and experiences for the player; and that won’t happen in an instant or just by simply telling the players to empathize. It takes time. And it takes mindfully curated experiences that the player goes through that slowly nurtures the growth of empathy in the player. But also to be honest, 

[08:58] the unified motivations are really not something absolutely necessary or heavily desired; And a detached player and character motivation also doesn’t directly mean a poor game or experience. But it is also not realistic to just put the players in a game’s plot and expect the character’s and player’s motivations to coincide. But if given time and put in effort, to unify the character and player motivations, or at least bring them a little closer, the curated experiences are more moving, satisfying and unforgettable.

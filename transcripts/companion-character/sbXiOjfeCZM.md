# Pythons and Martians and Finches, Oh My! Lessons Learned from a Mandatory 8th Grade Python Class
Amal Nanavati · 18 мин · 56 просмотров · en · https://youtu.be/sbXiOjfeCZM



[00:01] Hi all, and thank you for coming to this session! My name is Amal Nanavati, and today I will be talking about our experiences developing and teaching a mandatory 8th grade Python course. This is joint work with Aileen Owens at South Fayette School District, where the course is still being taught today, and Mark Stehlik at Carnegie Mellon University. Why do we need an 8th grade Python course? As we are know, there are numerous block-based programming curricula for the elementary school level, the most well-known of which is Scratch. There are also numerous text-based programming courses how the high school level, such as 

[00:35] the AP CS courses and Exploring Computer Science. However, there isn’t much in terms of a middle-school transition between the two. Further, as we are aware Computer Science and Computational Thinking are tools that can be applied to numerous different fields. This creates an opportunity for students, in high school, to use Computer Science concepts as part of other courses -- for example, to analyze data from a Chemistry lab, or to create an animation for a History project. However, this requires that students enter high school with a background in computer 

[01:07] science. To address both these needs, we developed an introductory Python course for 8th grade students, which out partner school district then made mandatory for all 8th graders. This presentation focuses on that course. We first describe the school district that we worked with, then describe the curriculum we created, then move on to the teacher’s experiences teaching a course to all students as opposed to those who were already interested in computer science, and finally present results 

[01:41] from pre- and post-surveys of students’ perspectives on Computer Science. For this project was a collaboration between Carnegie Mellon University and South Fayette School District, a suburban school district of 3,300 people near Pittsburgh. SFSD has defined computational thinking as a new literacy for all students, and has been working over the last 9 years to embed it into their K-12 curriculum. On the K-8 level, this includes block-based programming in Scratch and App Inventor, VexIQ 

[02:16] Robotics, and electrical circuity through Squishy Circuits. On the 9-12 level, they offer multiple elective text-based programming courses. However, they had noticed a gap between students’ interest and engagement with the block-based and text-based programming courses, which was one of the motivations to create this course. As 

[02:55] one way to achieve their computational thinking goals, South Fayette School District uses Incubator Courses, which are aftershcool courses that allow them to test new ideas and leverage external talent. The curriculum that we focus on was initially developed and taught by Teknowledge, a Carnegie Mellon student organization, as an incubator course in Spring 2017. We then worked with South Fayette School District to improve the course in summer 2017, and it has been taught as a mandatory course at the school district since fall 2017, with 

[03:30] roughly 25 students per class and 270 students per year. This course focuses on the 2018-19 year of teaching the course. This  course was one trimester, or 30 classes on a block-based schedule, with 40 minutes per class. It was roughly divided into three units, intended to expose students to the range of ways computer code can interact with the world. 

[04:02] These units were “command line,” “turtle,” and “finch robots.” Each class period began with some instruction, followed by guided practice where the teacher wrote code on the board and students followed along on their own laptops. Each class ended with students working on coding challenges, typically in pairs. Finally, the entire course was themed around The Martian, a film about an astronaut, Mart Watney, who gets stranded on Mars. At the beginning of the course, students are told that they are Mark Watney’s tech team, 

[04:34] and it is up to them to help Watney survive. Activities in every unit tie into this theme, and in the final unit, the Finch Robot is Watney’s Mars rover that students need to navigate around Mars. In the first unit, students learn the basics of Python code. They begin with print and strings, where they have sto send a help message to NASA. But they have to be careful; there is limited bandwidth on MARS, so they have to send the most information with the least characters possible. 

[05:06] They then move on to variables and numbers, where they create a variable for Watney’s remaining oxygen supply, and then string concatenation where they print how much oxygen is remaining. They then learn user input, and have to ask NASA how soon they can send rescuers. They then move on to mathematical operators, where they calculate how long Watney can survive on this level of oxygen, and finally conditionals, where they print a warning if the oxygen level is too low. This unit had two main activities. 

[05:38] Mad Libs, which students did after learning input and string concatenation, involved students asking their classmates to fill in the blanks of a story. And the Trivia Game, which students did after learning conditionals, involved students creating trivia puzzles for their classmates. 

[06:10] Here you see examples of student work from these two activities. That brings us to our second unit, turtle. turtle is a built-in module in Python that allows users to create a turtle and then move it around on screen, with commands such as move forward or turn right. As the turtle moves, it draws a line behind it. Enclosing a series of forward and turn commands in a loop allows students to create interesting geometric designs, as well as simulate the type of commands they would send to a robot. 

[06:45] We used the turtle to teach while and for loops. Students began by using loops with the command line, to, for example, print Watney’s remaining potatoes every day or print the number of days he has been on Mars. A student favorite activity, which was thought up by particularly inquisitive students, was to get their program to crash by looping forever We then moved onto the main activity of the unit, using the turtle to draw geometric patterns in the sand of Mars, so that a passing satellite could detect signs of life. 

[07:20] Challenges ranged from taking code that had been copied multiple times and replacing it with a for loop, to drawing a triangle or square, to drawing unique geometric patterns that allowed students to be creative. Here are some of the patterns students drew. Our third and final unit was the Robotics unit. This unit involves students controlling the Finch robot, which is the robot seen here, 

[07:53] created by Birdbrain technologies. Students are told that the Finch robot is Watney’s Mars rover. Watney’s satellite dish has blown away, and they have to send the Mars rover out to retrieve it. At this point, students have all the programming tools they need to use the Finch. Therefore, this unit focuses on a series of challenges. First, the students must get the Finch to move around. However, it might become nighttime while the rover is out. 

[08:39] Therefore, they have to use conditionals and the Finch’s light sensor to determine when it is dark outside, and turn on a light. Finally, they have to get the robot to systematically search an area in order to find the missing satellite. 

[09:18] In addition to all the aforementioned coding exercises, we also integrated various unplugged activities throughout the curriculum. The first activities students did was people pathing, where they paired up, one student closed their eyes, and the other student provided them very precise instructions to follow a path. The goal was to give students intuition about how clearly they have to specify every command to a computer, and to give them experience with the type of functions they would call when using turtle and the Finch. Another unplugged activity involved students coloring in a map of Watney’s surroundings, to give them experience with the discrete math aspects of computational thinking. Finally, at various points of the course we gave students unplugged code comprehension activities, where they had to identify what the code would print, or what error would come up if they ran the code. Since this was the first time our school district had taught a mandatory text-based programming course, we conducted a semi-structured interview with the teacher. Notably, teaching CS to all students -- as opposed to just those with prior interest -- was a novel experience for her as well, and she found that there was a high variance in students’ abilities and interests in CS. She felt that a couple of aspects of the course were crucial to accounting for the mandatory 

[09:51] nature of this course. First, she found that when students started losing ground in the course, they began giving up, and it was very hard to rekindle their interest. Therefore, she worked hard to ensure that students were not losing ground in the first place. This included relying on study hall, a flexible period at the end of every day where students could go to any class they needed extra help in. Study-hall allowed the teacher to interact one-on-one with students who did not understand the content or may have been too shy to raise their hand in class. 

[10:25] The teacher estimated that she worked with roughly 15% of the students in study hall. She also noticed that guided practice was a key to keeping students engaged. Guided practice allowed the teacher to highlight the code’s logic by first explaining it in English, which also helped the content seem more manageable. In addition, it demystified coding because the students could see the teacher make intentional bugs as she was typing and see the process she went through to debug her code. 

[10:57] On the curricular side, framing the course around the Martian helped keep it fun and exciting. The teacher also found that the large variety of in-class activities were effective at allowing the course to engage with all students. For example, MadLibs was interesting for students who liked writing, whereas Turtle was interesting for students who enjoyed drawing. Finally, she felt that the Robotics unit was important because it showed students a real-world impact of their code, it allowed students who were behind to catch up without the students 

[11:30] who were ahead getting bored, and it allowed students to get up and move around, which she felt helped dissipate their energy and keep them more focused. At the beginning and end of every trimester, students filled out a pre- and post survey that measured their confidence towards, interest in, and views about computer science. 

[12:05] All questions on this survey were measure on a five-point Likert scale, and we had a 71% completion rate of the survey, or 180 students. We analyzed the results using a repeated-measure ANOVA, followed by a paired t test for post-hoc testing. Our within-subjects factor was the time of the survey, and out between-subjects factor was gender and students’ prior familiarity with Python. We investigate seven different constructs, of which I will highlight two in this presentation: 

[12:38] Confidence and CS for Future. Our confidence questions were designed to measure how confident students were about their ability to learn and use computer science. This graph shows the pre-test results, where -2 is strongly disagree and 2 is strongly agree. 

[13:13] As you can see, there is a movement to the agree side between the pre- and post- surveys, and sure enough this different is significant. However, gender actually plays a role in this result. Amongst males, there was no significant difference between their confidence before and after taking the course, although amongst females there was a significant difference. Therefore, this course significantly lowered the gender gap in terms of students’ confidence towards Computer Science. 

[13:44] Our CS for Future questions, on the other hand, focused on student’s willingness to take further classes in or pursue a career in CS. Here are our pre- and post-survey results, and as you can see the proportion of students who disagreed with the statements decreased. Sure enough, that difference was significant. However, in this result, prior familiarity with Python plays a role. Those students who already knew Python had no significant change in their willingness 

[14:15] to engage with CS in the future, but those without prior familiarity with Python significant improved their willingness to re-engage with CS. This shows that our course significantly lowered the prior familiarity gap in terms of students’ willingness to engage with Computer Science in the future. We also asked students which parts of the course they wanted to spend more time on. As you can see, Print and conditionals were least popular, likely because those parts 

[14:53] mainly used the command line. To account for this, in future iterations of the course the teacher has started introducing the Finch earlier, to break up the monotony of the  command line unit. In addition, Graphics and the Finch units were popular across both genders. Finally, as we had mentioned, one of the goals of this course was to provide student’s 

[15:25] baseline CS knowledge that they could then use in high school. So how did the course actually affect high school course requests? Well, 2017-18 was the first time the course was offered, and in the subsequent year 9th grade requests roughly doubled, from 50 to 100. The following year, it increased by the same amount again, to roughly 150. 

[16:00] However, one thing to note is the gender ratio stayed roughly at 2 boys for every 1 girl, so the course clearly needs to do more to encourage girls to continue to engage with CS. Note that this effects actually persists beyond 9th grade. This graph shows high school CS course requests across all grad levels. The 9th grade data is what we had already seen going from 50 to 150 over two years. 

[16:34] However, if we look at 10th grade requests, they also increased from 62, for 10th graders who did not take the 8th grade course, to 101, for 10th graders who did. We are excited to see this trend pans out in future years of high school. In conclusion, we developed and taught a mandatory one-trimester Python course to all 8th grade 

[17:06] students in our school district. The curriculum was intended to balance core computer science skills with fun activities, such as Mad Libs and the Finch Robot. Teaching to all students presented unique challenges to the teacher, which she overcame with study hall, guided practice, and engaging activities. And finally, the course significantly improved students’ attitudes towards Computer Science as well as their high school Computer Science requests.

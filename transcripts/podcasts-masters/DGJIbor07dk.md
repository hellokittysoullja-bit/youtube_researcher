[01:07] One of the biggest mistakes I made early in my career was believing that design success was measured by how beautiful a screen looked. I spent hours selecting colors, gradients, shadows, and visual effects. The designs looked impressive, but something important was totally missing. Users don't open an application to admire the interface. They open it 

[01:38] because they want to complete a task. They want to transfer money. They want to buy a product. They want to find information. They want to solve a problem. If the interface looks amazing, but creates confusion, the design has failed then. Today, whatever I start a project, my first question is not "How should this look?" 

[02:10] Instead, I ask, "What problems are we trying to solve here?" That single shift in thinking changed my entire design approach. Many designers spend years improving technical skills. They learn Figma, they learn prototyping, they learn visual design. All of these skills are valuable. 

[02:42] But one skill that transformed my career was communication. I have seen excellent design solutions fail because expectations were not aligned. I have seen average solutions succeed because communication was clear. A designer spends a large portion of the day talking to product managers, developers, business 

[03:13] stakeholders, researchers, and users. If you cannot explain your design decisions clearly, people may not support them. Design is not only about creating solutions. It's also about helping others understand why those solutions matter. Early in my career, I often felt pressure to start designing immediately. 

[03:44] Everyone wanted screens quickly. Stakeholders wanted progress. Deadlines were tight. Research sometimes felt like a luxury. However, I eventually learned that skipping research often creates expensive mistakes later. When we make assumptions about users, we risk building the wrong solution. A few conversations with users can reveal issues we never expected. 

[04:15] Even small research activities can prevent weeks of redesign Today, I see research as an investment rather than a delay. The better we understand users, the better our design decisions become. This lesson became especially important as I started working on larger products and teams. Many designers initially see design system as limitations. 

[04:46] They think, "Why can't I design everything from scratch?" But after working on complex products, I discovered the exact opposite. Design systems actually create freedom. When common decisions are already standardized, designers can spend more energy solving real user problems. Design systems improve consistency. They improve collaboration with developers. They reduce design debt. 

[05:16] They increase speed. Most importantly, they help teams scale. A good design system is not about controlling creativity. It's about enabling better creativity. The design industry changes constantly. And during my career, I have seen tools rise, dominate, and eventually disappear. Technology change, processes change, trends change. 

[05:46] AI is now changing the industry again. If we become too attached to a specific tool, we risk falling behind. What has helped me most throughout my career is curiosity. Curiosity encourages learning. Curiosity encourages experimentation. Curiosity helps us adapt. The most successful designers I have met are not necessarily the most talented. 

[06:18] They are often the people who never stop learning. So, looking back at the last 13 years, these lessons have had the biggest impact on my career. Design is about solving problems.

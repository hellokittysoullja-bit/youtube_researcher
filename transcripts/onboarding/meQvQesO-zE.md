# SaaS Onboarding Best Practices: 6 UX Tips
Eleken · 4 мин · 589 просмотров · en · https://youtu.be/meQvQesO-zE



[00:00] What are the best practices for  SaaS onboarding? Here are the ones we use when designing onboarding at Eleken. First, get users to value as fast as possible. Your onboarding is not a product tour. Its job is  to get a new user to the moment where they think, "Okay, this is useful." So look at every step between signup and that moment. Do you really need their phone number right now? Their company size?  Ten setup questions? Credit card details? If a step doesn't help users experience  value, consider moving it later. Slack is a good example. Instead of  making you configure everything upfront, 

[00:32] it gets you into the workspace quickly. You  can create a channel, send a message, and start collaborating. Advanced configuration can wait.   Second, don't introduce every feature. This is one of the biggest  onboarding mistakes we see. Teams spend months building a product, so  naturally they want to show users everything. Don't. Find the two or three actions that demonstrate your product's  core value and build your onboarding around those. Grammarly does this well. It doesn't start by  explaining plagiarism detection, tone analysis, and every other feature. It gives you a  document with mistakes and lets you fix them. 

[01:06] You're using the product almost immediately. That's much more effective than clicking "Next" through twelve tooltips.   Which brings me to the third point: let users learn by doing. Passive product tours are easy to skip because users aren't accomplishing anything. Instead of saying, "This button creates a project," ask the user to  create their first project. Instead of explaining where analytics are,  help them generate their first report. Attention Insight redesigned its onboarding  around this idea. Users were guided through creating their first heatmap instead of just being  shown the interface. According to the case study, 

[01:39] activation increased by 47%.   Fourth, personalize the onboarding. A founder, a marketer, and a sales  manager may use the same SaaS product for completely different reasons. So why give them the same onboarding? Ask a few useful questions at signup. What's  their role? What are they trying to accomplish? How experienced are they? Then use those answers. Show different templates. Prioritize different  features. Create different paths to value. HubSpot does this with a short  onboarding survey and then adapts the workspace to the user's situation. But personalization doesn't mean 

[02:12] adding a twenty-question questionnaire before  someone can use your product. Keep it short. Fifth, don't teach everything upfront. Teach users when they need the information. We call this contextual or micro-onboarding. Think about Photoshop. Instead of explaining every tool when you first open the app, it can show  guidance when you encounter a particular tool. We've used the same principle at Eleken. When we designed Habstash, a budgeting product, some financial terms could be confusing for users.  Instead of explaining all of them in a giant tutorial, we added small contextual explanations  users could open when they needed them. 

[02:47] That's what good onboarding should  feel like: helpful, not intrusive. And sixth, measure what happens after you launch. Your first onboarding flow is an assumption. Watch where users drop off. Look at which actions  correlate with activation and retention. Talk to users. Then keep changing the flow. We did this when designing onboarding for Clientjoy. Before designing the experience, we talked to users and learned that newcomers needed two  things: an easy way to start and early confidence that they could actually use the product. So we designed a lightweight interactive 

[03:19] tour focused only on the actions users  needed first, like adding a customer, adding leads, and creating proposals.   That's an important distinction. Good SaaS onboarding starts  with good product design. You need to understand what value users came  for, remove everything standing between them and that value, and then design the  shortest useful path to get there. That's exactly how we approach onboarding at  Eleken. We specialize in SaaS product design, and we've worked on onboarding for products where  activation, adoption, and retention depend on 

[03:50] users understanding complex software quickly. So if users are signing up but disappearing before they understand why your product is  useful, don't immediately add more tooltips. * Find the friction. * Find the real aha moment. * Then redesign the journey around it.

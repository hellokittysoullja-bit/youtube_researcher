Throughout this video, we'll explore what dark patterns are, the different types you might encounter, and most importantly, how to avoid them in your own product designs. Dark patterns are interface tricks that mislead users into performing unwanted actions. They exploit psychological 

[00:30] biases and cognitive limitations to pressure users into making choices that benefit the company but harm the user. Whether it's signing up for services you don't want or spending money unexpectedly, dark patterns are everywhere in the digital world. Let's look at three common dark patterns. Roach Motel makes it easy to join but difficult to leave like subscriptions that require multiple steps to cancel. Bait and switch promises one thing but delivers 

[01:01] something completely different when you commit. Urgency creates fake scarcity or time pressure, making you rush decisions with phrases like only three items left or offer expires in 1 hour. Here are three more dark patterns to watch out for. Hidden costs reveal unexpected charges only at checkout after you've already decided to purchase. Trick questions use confusing wording or double negatives to make you agree to something you didn't intend. Sneaky subscriptions automatically renew 

[01:33] charges without clear user consent, often burying the terms in fine print. Three more patterns to be aware of. Social proof displays fake reviews or inflated user counts to influence your decisions. Visual interference makes important buttons like cancel or unsubscribe hard to find, often by making them small, gray, or hidden in menus. Confirm shaming uses negative language on reject buttons, like, "Are you sure you want to miss out on this amazing deal?" instead of simply saying, 

[02:05] "No thanks." When evaluating your product design, watch for these red flags. Pressure language like act now or only x left in stock creates artificial urgency. Hidden fine print buries important terms and conditions in small text, hoping users won't read them. If you notice your product relies on these tactics, it's time to redesign with user respect in mind. To create ethical products, start with 

[02:35] clear and transparent language. Use plain words that users actually understand, avoiding jargon and confusing terms. Equally important is making it easy to undo actions. If users can subscribe with one click, they should be able to unsubscribe just as easily. Simplify your cancellation process instead of creating obstacles. Continue building trust by respecting user choice. Treat accept and decline buttons equally, giving them the same visual prominence and size. Avoid making 

[03:07] the reject option look like a lesser choice. Also, commit to honest visual design. Don't hide buttons, use disguising colors, or create confusing layouts. Every element should be straightforward and transparent about its purpose. Why should you care about avoiding dark patterns? Building trust is essential. When users feel respected and valued by your product, they become loyal customers who return willingly. Sustainable growth comes from honest practices that create genuine user 

[03:39] engagement over time. Users may not immediately know why they prefer your product, but they'll sense that you respect them and that loyalty lasts far longer than any dark pattern trick ever could. Remember this fundamental truth. Great products don't need dark patterns. When you build something genuinely useful and user-friendly, people will use it because they want to, not because they've been tricked into it.

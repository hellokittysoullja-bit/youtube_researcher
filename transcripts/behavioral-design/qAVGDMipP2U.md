Friend spam. This happens when a platform asks for your email or social media access supposedly to help you find friends, but instead blasts spammy invites to everyone in your contacts, making it look like they came from you. LinkedIn famously did this, tricking users into connecting contacts, which led to millions of 

[03:07] unwanted invites being sent out. It backfired hard. They ended up with a $13 million lawsuit. Companies use this tactic because messages from friends feel more trustworthy. If it looks like someone you know is inviting you, you're more likely to sign up. And it works because it hijacks trust. People don't question something that seems personal. Hidden subscription. This is when users are unknowingly enrolled in a recurring payment plan without clear disclosure. A good example is Figma. For a long time, if you invited a teammate and 

[03:38] accidentally gave them editing access instead of view only, you'd automatically get charged for a paid seat. The modal didn't clearly warn you that this action would increase your bill. Companies use this tactic because it turns what feels like a free or lowcost product into a steady revenue stream. It works because people assume they'll get a warning before being charged or they forget to cancel. By the time they notice the surprise bill, the payment has already gone through. Sneak into basket. This happens when something gets 

[04:08] added to your shopping cart without you clearly choosing it. For example, airlines used to sneak in extras like travel insurance or seat upgrades by pre-checking boxes. So, unless you noticed and removed it, you paid more. Companies do this to boost their average order value without needing you to consciously opt in. It works because many people skim checkout screens and assume everything is standard. You don't notice until after you've paid. Misdirection. This is when a layout is designed to 

[04:38] draw your attention one way while hiding what really matters. A classic example is giant accept all buttons for cookies while the decline option is hidden in gray text. They do this because most people will take the path of least resistance. And it works because our brains are wired to notice contrast, size, and color. If the wrong choice is made more obvious, our instincts pull us there before logic catches up. Price comparison prevention. [05:41] or slightly more expensive option, the so-called goldilocks effect that bumps average revenue per customer. It works because most of us don't want to spend 30 minutes analyzing plan details. If one option seems safer or better, even without full clarity, we'll often just choose it and move on. Privacy Zuckering, named after Mark Zuckerberg. Urgency or scarcity, only two left in stock or sale ends in 5 minutes. You've seen these before. They're designed to trigger panic buying. Companies use urgency and scarcity because when we think something is about to run out, we act fast instead of weighing our options. It works because of loss aversion. We fear missing out more than we value making the perfect choice. 

[07:17] Sometimes scarcity is real, like concert tickets with limited seats. But often it's manufactured urgency, nudging people into decisions they wouldn't make if they had time to think.

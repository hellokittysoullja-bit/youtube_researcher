For example, some mobile games ask you to rate them out of five stars. If  you pick five, you’re sent straight to the app store to leave a review. [02:39] They may not have committed to spending  as much if they knew that at the start. Dark patterns in games extend far beyond  just deceptive user interfaces though. They're typically categorised as temporal, social,  psychological and monetary dark patterns. Before we get started, I should note that  pretty much any mechanism that tries to manipulate a change in user behaviour  is considered a dark pattern in games, but the severity varies a lot. Some  are much more mild than others, 

[03:11] and we'll all have different opinions about  which of them are harmless and which are going too far. I'll weigh in with my personal  feelings about this later in the video. Many live service games have daily login  rewards. In behavioural psychology, when an action is followed by a reward, your  brain learns to repeat it. Every day that you log in and receive this reward, you get a  hit of dopamine, building a habit over time. Players who are conditioned to logging in every  day do it not because they’re excited to play, but because it feels like brushing their  teeth: just something they have to do. Some games have login streaks. This is where the  rewards escalate for each sequential day you log 

[03:45] in. When you break the streak, your rewards reset  back down to the minimum level. Now, humans have a strong sense of loss aversion, where losses feel  roughly twice as bad as gains feel good, and hence players will go significantly out of their way  to avoid breaking these streaks. As I mentioned before, I have a friend who was on an off-the-grid  camping trip and had to walk six kilometres with his laptop and a 3G modem so that he could keep  a login streak going in some random game he was playing. There are even games out there that let  you pay real money to restore a broken streak. In addition to daily login bonuses, many  games have daily tasks or challenges, 

[04:19] which reset every 24 hours. Players feel obliged  to complete these, so that the easy rewards don't go to waste. They don't want to miss out on  cheap progress that everyone else is getting. Some games rely on dark patterns rooted in  social obligation to manipulate you into behaving differently. For example, it's common for mobile  base-builder games to have guilds or clans where daily participation like doing quests or donating  resources is important for your guild to succeed. If you don't log in each day and perform these  actions, you let your friends down, which causes guilt and social pressure for you to be active  daily. This dark pattern can exploit and strain 

[04:53] our friendships, especially when the game is tuned  to make daily participation essentially mandatory. Other games feature gifting between players,  where reciprocation of gifts is rewarded with extra bonuses. This taps into reciprocity  pressure to cause you to feel the need to log in daily and return gifts to people.  Some games have refer-a-friend bonuses, or even outright pyramid schemes where you  make faster progress if you have recruited an extensive network of friends to  join the game and play with you. Time-gated tasks, or "playing by appointment"  are when a game tells you that you have to come 

[05:25] back at a specific time, or face consequences.  Popularised by farming games where your crops would wither and die if not harvested at the right  time, the game forces control of your schedule, sometimes making you wake up in the middle of the  night to click buttons, or lose your investment. Contemporary mobile games often have timers  and energy mechanics, where your ability to progress the game is blocked until you come  back when these resources have regenerated. While this is less harsh than your crops  being lost, you'll feel pressure to come back at the right time to maximise your  rate of progress. When you're not the 

[05:56] one deciding when you play a game, but  instead having the game decide for you, it's worth questioning your entire  relationship with that game. Some live service games create artificial  scarcity for their store items. As entirely digital purchases, there's no physical requirement  to limit the number of people who can purchase something, but doing so often causes it to sell  much faster due to players fearing that they're going to miss out. This pattern is similar  to the one employed in Gacha games, where they often have certain characters  available for purchase only occasionally, causing a frenzy of activity while they are. Not  only does this cause more sales, but it conditions 

[06:30] players to check in with the game daily to see  if the character they want is available for sale. There are a variety of dark patterns related to  premium currency in games. Developers can design their premium currency packs to be slightly  too small to buy the most desirable items, requiring players to purchase more  premium currency at once than they planned to. Developers can pick arbitrary  exchange rates for their premium currency, so that players can't really understand the  true cost of the item they're buying. Cheap items might be placed next to expensive items in  the store to make them look like better value. 

[07:01] Some games employ special in-game or monetisation  events that are time-limited or seasonal. The content and rewards are only available for a  limited time window. Players do not want to miss a unique reward that may never be available  again, so this limited availability causes fear of missing out, which leads to intense  and anxious play during the event window. There's also a phenomenon called the Endowed  Progress Effect, which shows that if people are given a head start on a reward track, then they  are far more likely to finish the rest of that track so that they don't waste the progress they  have so far, even though they didn't even make 

[07:32] that progress themselves. It's a bit like the sunk  cost fallacy. This is why some battle pass schemes start with the first items already completed,  or have some really easy ones that are completed inadvertently while playing. This manipulates  players into committing to complete a reward track that they may not have chosen to attempt  without the bump of free progress at the start. So we've gone through a bunch of different  dark patterns, and some of them seem pretty user-hostile. You may be wondering, why would  developers pollute their live service games with all this crap? Well, there are several answers  to that question, and the first is that it works. 

[08:05] These techniques are all highly effective. They  produce the desired outcomes. They manipulate well-understood psychological principles that  our human brains are vulnerable to. [09:11] and monetisation is through meta systems, which  very easily stray into dark pattern territory. Finally, the entire games-as-a-service industry  measures the value of a game as a function of its Daily Active User count and its retention  rates. If you're making a live service game, you're only going to be able to get funding  if you have a solid plan to retain your users.

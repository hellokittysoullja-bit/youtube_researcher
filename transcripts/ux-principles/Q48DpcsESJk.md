Let's start with Hick's Law because that's the technical backbone behind a lot of UX advice about simplifying interfaces. Hick's Law, also called the Hick-Hyman Law, comes from mid-20th century experiments in psychology that measured how long it takes people to make a choice when they see different numbers of options. In 1952, British psychologist William Edmund Hick published research showing that choice reaction time increases in a very 

[01:02] specific way as you add more stimulus-response alternatives. And Ray Hyman followed up with related work in 1953, which is why you sometimes hear the combined name Hick-Hyman Law. At the core of Hick's Law sits a simple equation with big implications for design. The relationship between decision time and number of choices is usually written as T = A + B log 2 N, where T is reaction time, N is the number of choices, A stands for base reaction time, and B represents how much 

[01:32] extra time each bit of information adds. What this means in plain language is that when you double the number of options, the time cost increases, but not in a straight line because the law uses a logarithm with base 2. But here's where things get really interesting. If Hick's Law is correct, then we should be able to see its effects everywhere in everyday life, not just inside websites and mobile apps. Think about walking into a grocery store. Imagine one shelf contains six different 

[02:03] brands of jam. Most people can quickly compare them and make a choice. Now imagine another shelf containing 60 nearly identical jars. Suddenly, the decision becomes harder. You start comparing ingredients, prices, flavors, reviews, and even packaging. The extra options don't necessarily make the decision better. They simply require more mental effort. The same thing happens in digital products. Early websites often believed that 

[02:34] giving users more options was always beneficial. Designers packed navigation bars with links, drop-down menus with dozens of categories, and home pages with endless buttons. The thinking was simple. If everything is visible, users will find what they need. But real-world behavior showed something different. When users face too many choices at once, they spend more time scanning the screen, more time evaluating possibilities, [music] and more time wondering whether they are about to click the wrong thing. 

[03:06] As a result, many modern interfaces deliberately hide complexity. Notice how smartphone operating systems group apps into folders. Notice how e-commerce sites use categories and filters instead of displaying every product at once. Notice how streaming platforms recommend a handful of titles rather than presenting their entire library on the first screen. These design decisions are not accidental. They were attempts to reduce cognitive load. Cognitive load refers to the 

[03:37] amount of mental effort required to process information. Human attention is limited, and every choice competes for a portion of that attention. When too many options appear at the same time, users can begin to feel mentally exhausted before they even make a decision. This creates an important challenge for UX designers. Removing options completely is not always the answer. After all, users still want flexibility [music] and control. The real goal is organization. 

[04:09] For example, imagine an online clothing store with 1,000 products. Showing all 1,000 items on a single page would be overwhelming. But dividing them into categories such as shirts, jackets, shoes, and accessories immediately makes the experience easier. The total number of products hasn't changed. What changed is how the choices are presented. This idea highlights an important distinction. Users don't necessarily struggle because there are many options. They struggle 

[04:39] when many options are presented in a confusing way. That's why successful [music] interfaces often guide people through decisions step-by-step. Instead of asking users to make one giant decision, they break the process into smaller decisions. Choose a category, then choose a product type, then choose a specific item. Each step feels manageable, even though the overall number of possibilities remains large. This approach appears almost everywhere in the digital world, even if most users 

[05:09] never consciously notice it. You can see it when shopping online. Instead of presenting thousands of products on a single page, e-commerce platforms guide users through categories, filters, recommendations, and search tools. The goal is not to reduce the number of available products. The goal is to make the decision-making process feel manageable. You can see it in streaming services as well. Platforms may have tens of thousands of movies and shows in their libraries, yet they rarely present all of them at once. 

[05:42] Instead, they organize content into genres, personalized recommendations, trending lists, and curated collections. [music] By breaking a massive choice into smaller, more digestible groups, they make it easier for users to find something worth watching. At this point, you might be thinking about all those times you opened a streaming app, scrolled for several minutes, and then gave up without picking a movie. That's where the paradox of choice comes into the story. Psychologist Barry Schwartz brought this 

[06:14] phrase into the mainstream with his book The Paradox of Choice: Why More Is Less, which Swarthmore College's repository describes as an explanation of how the hallmark of individual freedom, having many options, can start to undermine psychological and emotional well-being. Unlike Hick's Law, which directly measures decision time, the paradox of choice digs into emotions and regret [music] after the decision. Resources like Model Thinkers describe two outcomes that line up with everyday 

[06:44] experience. Choice paralysis and lower happiness. Choice paralysis shows up when the number of options climbs so high that people simply freeze and walk away rather than commit to a single decision, even if some of those options are objectively good. However, the story is not as simple as more options are always bad. A 2010 meta-analysis by Scheibehenne and colleagues looked across many experiments on choice overload and found that the average effect of having many options was very small and not reliably 

[07:14] negative. The result was a shift in the conversation, where we researchers started to stress that context matters, including factors like how important the decision feels, how familiar the person is with the domain, and how clearly the options are presented. Bringing it back to our premise, this is the story of how Hick's Law and the paradox of choice shaped UX design by giving designers a language for what users feel when they [music] face crowded screens and endless choices. When you combine these two ideas with 

[07:45] modern research that challenges simplistic myths, you end up with a more balanced mindset. One where UX design is not only about cutting options, but about presenting choices in a way that respects both the mind's limits and the emotions that follow every decision.

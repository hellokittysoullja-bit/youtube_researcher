# Laws of UX Design: The von Restorff Effect

[00:00] In 1933 Hedwig von Restorff, a German psychologist and pediatrician published her paper “On the effects of the formation of a structure in the trace field” and, as a first one, reported that memory is better for isolated than for non, isolated pieces of information, a phenomenon long known as the von Restorff or Isolation effect. In simple terms, anything which stands out from a group of similar elements will focus our attention on that element. This is very useful for us designers, because we can highlight key actions we want users 

[00:32] to perform. Thousands of years of human evolution equipped us with a keen sense of vision and recognition. We are able to recognise items in a split of a second, and in comparison to animals we are also able to unravel patterns and even the smallest differences between them. In archaic times those differences were crucial for our survival and we are still using them today. They are affecting the way we see and process surrounding reality. Our instincts affect how we store information in our memory, thereby they also affect our 

[01:02] ability to recall things and events, where recognition has priority over memory itself. You can check this out for yourself. Find an old magazine in your house that you read in the past. Probably you will recognise what's on its pages with “Oh, I remember this”, but this is just an illusion, where your mind is playing tricks on you. You can verify it by trying to recall what's on the next page. Most likely you won’t be able to, because you recognise the magazine and its pages but you don’t really remember the content itself. 

[01:35] In the context of interface design, contrast has the most important effect on directing users attention. Our primary challenge as designers is to manage what elements of the UI users should focus on, at the same time assisting them with achieving their goals. We can use the difference in color, shape or size to focus users' attention on a pathway to their goals, but we need to be very careful, because excess of contrasting elements can have the opposite effect and be super confusing. That's why a typical good practice is to highlight only key actions or preferred ones. 

[02:07] Since we cover the concept behind the von Restorff effect I think it is a good idea to mention another equally important concept connected to it, which is selective attention. In modern times we are bombarded every day with many different things that want our attention. Our phones, TVs, computers, and many other things are competing with each other for it. Our ability to focus on something has dropped like crazy in the last 10 years to the point where the average human attention span is now compared by many to that of a goldfish, 

[02:37] which is about 8 to 9 seconds. In order to focus on something, we often ignore other things that are around us, filtering them in a sense. A great example of that filtering in the context of UI and UX design is banner blindness, a type of selective attention where users ignore anything which looks similar to advertisement. When we consider banner blindness in context of our attention span it's obvious that users will ignore everything which is not useful to them at the moment of achieving their tasks through our products. Our attention is a limited resource that's why we often ignore anything which doesn’t 

[03:11] look important in order to achieve our tasks. It is our job as designers to direct users' attention to the proper elements of our interfaces. I have already mentioned the general rules of achieving this through color, shape or size, now let me show you how it works through examples. As you can see on the first design, there is no distinction between actions where in comparison to the other one, the core function is highlighted. Lack of contrast can potentially lead users to perform the wrong action, visual clue not only helps to achieve user goal but also prevents them from selecting the wrong option. 

[03:46] We can often find von Restorff effect in price tables, where the preferred option is highlighted in some way to focus potential clients attention. This is where the Isolation effect is not only applicable in Design but also in behavioral economics or consumer psychology. Concepts behind this effect can be applied not only to the individual UI elements. As you can see in this example you can achieve visual contrast in many different ways. Scale, shape, empty space or movement could be also utilized to help you highlight selected 

[04:17] elements or contents from surrounding information. Isolation effects allow you to elevate your design practice to a higher level, especially that you now know details that are driving it. But, remember to not overuse it in your interfaces, so you won’t end up on the bad side of it.

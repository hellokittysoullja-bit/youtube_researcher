In fact, the secret of Duolingo lies in the fact that they use psychology and behavioral science to keep us learning. Let's start with habits. A habit is an action we perform daily and unconsciously. For example, drinking coffee in the morning , brushing your teeth, or exercising. We don't ask ourselves the question: should I do this? We just go ahead and do it. That is why turning actions into a habit is the number one goal for any 

[01:01] digital business. And Duolingo managed to do this for millions of users. To find out how, let's take a closer look at the mechanics of a habit. A habit consists of three parts. The first is the trigger, a signal that prompts the user to take action. The second is the habit itself, the routine, the action. And the third is the reward the user receives for completing the action. Let's figure out how Duolingo uses this process to build a habit. And we will start with triggers. Triggers signal that it is time to perform some action. For example, you wake up in the morning and go to the bathroom to brush your 

[01:32] teeth. Not because you want to brush your teeth, but because you do it every day. Duolingo uses several triggers to get us to open the app. These are push notifications and widgets, email reminders, and also emotional manipulation. One of the more common ways to form habits in an app is push notifications. Especially those that leverage psychological triggers like loss aversion, scarcity, commitment, and consistency. However, when using push notifications, a pitfall arises: users must allow the app to display 

[02:03] push notifications. While most apps simply politely ask their users to allow them to show push notifications, Duolingo gives a reward for this in the form of a chest. Users receive a chest with in-app bonuses that can be opened somewhere between 6:00 PM and midnight. And Duolingo asks if users want to receive a notification that the chest can already be opened. This is a win-win strategy. Users get a small reward in the form of a chest with bonuses, and the app gets access to push notifications. Another interesting Duolingo trigger is the fear of breaking your streak. And it really 

[02:34] works. I have a streak of about 300 days on Duolingo myself. And it’s really a shame to lose it now. Users also receive a message, or I’d even say, a proposal from Duolingo. To add a widget to your home screen with the number of days in your streak. This makes the app more visible and also increases the chance that you’ll start a lesson. Email reminders. The creators of Duolingo understand that if a user doesn't react to a push notification, the next logical step is to ping them via email. Their emails are very simple, logical, and effective 

[03:06] . They include a subject line with specific phrasing like: "Hi Nikita, please spend 5 minutes learning English ." A simple layout with a clear call to action and a "Start Lesson" button, plus personalized info about your streak and why you shouldn't break it, which serves as another prompt to act. Emotional manipulation. Duolingo is known for its green owl character named Duo. He has quite a vibrant personality . It’s a charming mascot that the brand uses to manipulate the emotions and actions of its audience. For example, if you don't use the app for a while, you’ll start getting emails 

[03:37] like: "You’ve upset Duo, make Duo happy." Even though the company dialed it back after the audience's outraged reaction and numerous memes, it's still quite hard to ignore such messages. Playing on users 'sense of guilt is a controversial point, but for Duolingo, it works perfectly. Duolingo’s design is teeming with psychological strategies that encourage users to get drawn into language learning. Pre-commitments, social motivation, streaks, the goal-gradient effect and chunking, variable rewards, XP boosts, and treasure chests. These are all 

[04:08] strategies that Duolingo uses. And let’s look at them in more detail. Pre-commitment is a motivational strategy where the core idea is that people promise to do something in the future. Studies show that by making a commitment, we feel the need to remain consistent and are less likely to procrastinate. The more specific and achievable the pre-commitment is, the more likely we are to follow through. Duolingo asks users to set a clear goal before they start learning. First, they are asked to promise to spend 3, 5, or 

[04:39] 10-15 minutes a day studying, and then to specify the desired number of days of continuous learning: 7, 14, 30, 60, and so on. If you want to add pre-commitment to your interface, follow Duolingo’s example and set specific goals. For instance, let’s take the design of a fitness app. Don't ask the user, "Do you want to work out? " with answers like "Yes" or "Maybe," but rather provide much more specific options. Be more specific: do you want to exercise on our machine 3 or 7 days a week? This way, you avoid abstraction and give the user a specific number of 

[05:09] days that is easy to evaluate. Social motivation or competition increases physical endurance, adherence to good habits, and even reaction speed. In Duolingo, this principle is implemented in the form of a leaderboard. As soon as you reach a high enough spot on the table, you advance to the next league. And there are quite a lot of leagues, which keeps users interested. A leaderboard is a proven way to maintain audience engagement, but it is not suitable for all projects. For example, it is inappropriate for apps that count days without alcohol or cigarettes. The risk of failing in public will do more 

[05:41] harm than good. Think carefully about whether such competition aligns with your goals, brand, and audience. You can maintain user engagement not only through promotions and rewards. You also need to clearly demonstrate their progress, including how many lessons are left ahead. The goal gradient effect states that a user is motivated more by how much is left to do than by how much they have already done. That is why, when a cafe stamps your loyalty card, your motivation to get a free coffee increases. Education at the Pajama Web Design Academy is built the same way. All our lessons are broken 

[06:11] down into special modules so that learning goes steadily and you aren't overwhelmed with info, asking: "What do I do with this?" If you want to learn how to create trendy and user-friendly interfaces, we invite you to study at the Pajama Web Design Academy. The goal gradient effect also works in Duolingo. The app takes a vast cloud of knowledge , breaks it into topics and subtopics, and clearly shows the beginning and end of the path. Duolingo tracks how many days in a row you have practiced your chosen language and reminds you every time you open the app. This motivates 

[06:41] you to continue learning without skipping so as not to break your streak . However, the streak also has a downside when a user finally breaks it. It is very difficult to motivate them to start building the streak again from scratch. That is why Duolingo provides the option to freeze a streak. If you have completed the task for a certain number of days, you can miss one day, it will be frozen, and the streak will continue to count. Social norms are behaviors and beliefs that are considered normal in society. At a subconscious level, we all orient ourselves toward the actions of other people. Duolingo knows this and shows users how many other people are 

[07:12] learning the language along with them. However, social norms are not that simple. Depending on how they are presented, they can lead to different consequences. For example, if your goal is to reduce the number of students who drink on weekends and you decide to hand out flyers saying something like " 40%of students drink on weekends, don't be one of them." At first glance, this idea seems successful, but in reality, students might subconsciously internalize that more people consume alcohol than they thought, meaning such behavior is socially exemplary. But if 

[07:44] we flip this statistic and say that 60% don't drink alcohol on weekends, the social norm suddenly transforms into the idea that consuming alcohol is unacceptable. Here, I can recommend a great book, "Thinking, Fast and Slow," which will help you avoid cognitive biases and manipulation and evaluate situations critically. A reward is the benefit you receive for performing habitual actions. For example, coffee in the morning gives you a boost of energy, great taste, sugar, fats— everything you need for a good start to the day. In the Duolingo app, there are many rewards that make users feel 

[08:16] simply amazing after every correct answer. Duolingo uses several ways to reward its users. These are celebrations, experience points and quests, experience boosts and various chests, as well as achievements and badges. Every time you successfully complete a lesson, Duo the owl and another character from the Duolingo universe perform a celebratory dance. It's a small thing, but celebrating small victories like this can significantly increase audience engagement. We subconsciously experience pleasure when we hear or see something that confirms our views or actions. Therefore, when users finish a 

[08:47] lesson and see a cute dancing character , they understand that they did something right, that they completed the task, and they can truly rejoice. Continuous reinforcement is one of the gamification tactics, which implies that users receive a reward every time they perform an action. In Duolingo, the reward is expressed in experience points, which the user receives for every successfully completed lesson. To keep earning experience from being too predictable, Duolingo also uses variable rewards in the form of chests or double XP potions that last for 15 minutes, which encourages the user to 

[09:19] complete another lesson and stay in the app. And chests are an excellent trigger for opening the app. If you enter the app just to get a chest, the probability that you will complete a lesson is much higher than if you didn't enter the app at all. Achievements and badges are one of the most common gamification elements in mobile apps. Digital icons and badges in Duolingo are no exception. Such badges maintain engagement and encourage users to keep moving forward. Badges are an essential element of gamification, and they likely really 

[09:50] work, as an IBM study showed that 87%of users who received a virtual badge were more engaged in the process, and 76%of companies noted that such badges motivate employees more. And if you, like me, love all kinds of cool pins and badges in real life, then you will definitely fall under the influence of badges in apps. Duolingo also makes the process of learning languages contagious. Social connections and networking are an important factor in the growth of an app's audience. Duolingo users can share their achievements and successes on social 

[10:22] networks, as well as add friends, meet new people, and compete. This is a whole system of psychological solutions and strategies in one application.

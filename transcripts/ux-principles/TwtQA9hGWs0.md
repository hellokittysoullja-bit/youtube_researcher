Every repetition helps users practice a concept, a task, or an interaction. So, if your site uses a standard interaction pattern that people have encountered before on thousands of other sites, you're taking advantage

It's also easier to innovate if your users will experience the new interface very often, perhaps several times a day. That means that people will have a lot of practice with your design in a short amount of time. Innovation can also happen if designers adopt it en masse and create a new standard.

Usually, such a process takes time, but it did happen with design elements such as the swipe to delete gesture in iOS or the hamburger menu on mobile. If you're considering defining a new UI pattern 

[02:35] in cases where a conventional design is already well established, ask yourselves, first, will the new design perform much better than the old once users have had the necessary practice? In other words, is there an advantage for users if they transition to the new design? If not, don't even try. Second, is it credible that users will be willing 

[03:05] to try the new design again and again until they have learned it well enough to realize those long-term benefits? If people are likely to give up, for instance, leave a website for a competing familiar design, then don't introduce the new design. And third, can you speed up learning either by exposing users to the new design more often or by making it easier to learn 

[03:37] with contextual tips and other kinds of support. If yes, you will increase the proportion of users who will be willing to embrace the new design. So yes, consistency is the curse of innovation and design. Think twice at what you're trying to achieve. Is it worth departing from the well beaten path? Does it make sense to innovate, or will you be just as well served by a traditional design? 

[04:10] If you're convinced that once your users will have learned a new pattern, they will save time over the status quo, then it can be worth trying.
